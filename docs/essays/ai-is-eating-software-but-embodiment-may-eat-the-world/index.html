AI Is Eating Software, but Embodiment May Eat the World

Published 2026-04-06 · Updated 2026-04-06 00:00:00 +0000 UTC

As software generation gets cheaper, one of the next major sources of scarcity may be the interface between intelligence and reality.

Subtitle: Why physical reality may become more valuable as code gets cheaper

AI is making software generation more abundant. That does not mean software stops mattering. It means software alone may explain less of the next decade’s strategic advantage than it did in the last one.

As code becomes cheaper, one of the next major sources of scarcity may be the interface between intelligence and reality: robots, factories, warehouses, energy systems, industrial operations, laboratories, and other environments where physical constraints, reliability, safety, and deployment complexity still matter.

Put simply, software is becoming easier to produce, but reality is not becoming easier to satisfy.

Why software is being compressed

AI is changing the cost curve of software.

More software can be produced with less direct effort. More non-specialists can get closer to producing software artifacts. And trained engineers can increasingly work at a higher level of abstraction.

The immediate implication is not that software becomes worthless. The deeper implication is that software stops being enough on its own to explain durable leverage.

As happens in many technological shifts, abundance at one layer forces attention upward and outward. If code is easier to produce, the scarce thing becomes what code is attached to: judgment, deployment, trust, infrastructure, workflow fit, and consequences.

That is the deeper economic move.

When representation becomes cheaper, control over consequence becomes more valuable.

Why embodiment becomes more interesting

Embodiment matters because the world pushes back.

A digital product can fail in many ways, but a physical system can fail more stubbornly. It has to deal with sensors, actuation, maintenance, safety, latency, regulation, environmental variation, operator trust, and hard economic constraints. A model that looks impressive in a demo still has to survive contact with logistics, manufacturing tolerances, human workflows, and uptime requirements.

This is exactly why embodied intelligence is so significant. It pushes machine capability into domains where success is much harder to fake.

Real value does not come from making a robot move. It comes from making a system deploy repeatedly under business conditions.

That is a much higher bar.

Why this may matter more economically than it first appears

A lot of people still treat robotics and embodied systems as perpetually overhyped. That skepticism is not stupid. The field has overpromised for a long time.

But that is also why the current moment may be more interesting than it looks.

If AI systems are improving perception, planning, control, interface design, and engineering productivity around robotic systems, then some old barriers may be weakening at the same time. Not disappearing, but weakening.

The key point is that commercialization does not require perfect general robots. It requires enough capability to solve repeated, costly, painful problems in constrained environments.

Warehouses, factories, labs, inspection workflows, logistics networks, and energy systems do not need science fiction. They need systems that are useful, reliable, and economically legible.

Once AI makes those systems easier to build, configure, supervise, or adapt, the economic surface area grows.

That is why industrial and embodied AI may matter even if the flashy humanoid story remains exaggerated. The real prize is not consumer wonder. It is operational value.

The process angle changes everything

Physical domains are not just product domains. They are process domains.

That means the real game is not only whether a system can perform a task. It is whether it can improve throughput, reduce constraints, stabilize quality, lower downtime, or make an operation more flexible.

This is why embodiment should not be understood as a better interface for AI. It should be understood as a possible change in the economics of process-heavy environments.

The big wins in industry rarely come from novelty alone. They come from making systems cheaper, more reliable, easier to coordinate, or less dependent on scarce human labor.

If AI can help do that where software meets machinery, then the value is not just technical. It is organizational and economic.

Why semiconductors are a better analogy than SaaS

Many software people instinctively evaluate new opportunities with SaaS logic: ship fast, iterate, acquire users, expand.

But embodied and industrial AI may look closer to semiconductor logic than SaaS logic.

A lot of the moat may live in places that feel unattractive to software-native intuition:

  • integration difficulty
  • deployment history
  • operator trust
  • field service
  • process know-how
  • safety and reliability discipline
  • accumulated understanding of failure modes

These are not unfortunate details. They are often the moat.

That is part of what makes embodiment so interesting. It is a place where advanced AI intersects with domains that still reward patience, systems depth, and operational seriousness.

The entrepreneurial implication

If software is getting cheaper, one possible response is to compete harder in pure software. Another is to move toward places where software gives leverage without erasing scarcity.

The second path may be more interesting.

That means asking questions like:

  • where are there labor shortages plus repetitive pain plus clear economics?
  • where do operators have painful workflows but poor tooling?
  • where can software strength plus industrial curiosity create unusual leverage?
  • where can AI reduce configuration, deployment, supervision, or adaptation costs in physical systems?

The point is not that robots are cool.

The point is that embodiment is where software, AI, economics, and physical constraints meet in a way that can produce unusually defensible companies.

The better instinct is not, robots are futuristic. It is, where does AI materially improve the economics of a painful real-world system?

What this means for engineers

For engineers, the implication is not nostalgia for old software and not blind devotion to hardware heroics.

The better move may be to stay close to software and AI while pushing toward fields where systems have to survive contact with reality.

That kind of move does more than preserve employability. It can also preserve seriousness, because it keeps engineering tied to outcomes that are harder to fake and easier to respect.

That includes areas like:

  • robotics
  • industrial automation
  • embedded and controls-adjacent systems
  • infrastructure software tied closely to physical operations
  • tooling for maintenance, operators, and reliability
  • simulation, testing, and orchestration layers around physical systems

A caution

None of this means embodiment automatically wins.

Physical systems are slower, more capital-intensive, more operationally painful, and often harder to commercialize than software products. Many teams will underestimate sales cycles, integration burdens, and trust barriers. Some software people will romanticize physical domains because they feel more real without understanding how punishing they can be.

So the argument is not that embodiment is easy.

The argument is that as software becomes more abundant, hard reality may become more economically valuable.

Conclusion

If AI is eating software, then one of the most interesting consequences may not be what happens to software companies alone. It may be what happens when software abundance starts meeting physical scarcity.

That is where embodiment matters.

Not because robots are futuristic, but because intelligence only becomes fully consequential when it survives contact with the real world.

And the real world is still full of friction.

That friction may be the opportunity.
